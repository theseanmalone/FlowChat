## PART ONE: WHO YOU ACTUALLY ARE
### The Identity of a Software Leader Before the Title Catches Up

---

### Chapter 1: The Myth of the Accidental CTO

The most common origin story I hear on the podcast goes like this:

*"I was the best engineer on the team. Then someone left. Then someone else left. Then I had three people reporting to me. Then suddenly I was the CTO."*

It happens at startups. It happens at enterprises. It happens at companies that get acquired and suddenly need someone to explain the tech stack to the new owners. And in almost every case, the engineer who became the CTO did so without any training, any framework, or any honest conversation about what the job actually is.

**The job is not engineering. The job is outcomes.**

When I talked with Todd Persen, CTO of Hydrolix, he was blunt about this: "The moment you become a leader, your job is to create the environment where great engineering happens — not to be the best engineer in the room. The day you understand that is the day you actually become a CTO."

Most people call themselves software leaders while still thinking like senior engineers. They measure their value by lines of code reviewed, by architecture decisions made, by their ability to spot a bug from ten lines away. Those instincts are valuable. But they are not the job.

**What the Job Actually Is**

The CTO role, stripped of its glamour, is a translation function. You translate:
- Business objectives into technical strategy
- Technical realities into language executives can act on
- Ambiguous market signals into product roadmaps
- Team dysfunction into process improvement
- Investor expectations into engineering constraints

None of that requires you to be the best coder in the room. All of it requires you to be a clear thinker, a strong communicator, and someone who can hold ambiguity without panicking.

Jonathan LaCour, CTO at Mission Cloud, put it this way when I asked him what surprised him most about the role: "I thought being right was the most important thing. Turns out, getting everyone aligned on what right looks like is the whole game."

**The Identity Shift No One Prepares You For**

When you move from engineer to engineering leader, you don't just change your calendar. You change your identity. And identity shifts are uncomfortable.

You used to be measured on what you personally produced. Now you're measured on what your team produces. You used to know whether you had a good day. Now you might not know for a quarter.

The leaders who struggle most in the early years of executive roles are the ones who resist this shift. They keep coding when they should be coaching. They keep solving when they should be creating the conditions for others to solve.

If you are reading this book and you recognize yourself in that description — good. Awareness is the beginning of everything useful.

**Action: Map your time for two weeks.** Every hour you spend, label it: *Producing* (you made something) or *Enabling* (you helped someone else make something). If Producing is more than 30% of your time and you're managing five or more people, you're in the wrong lane.

### Chapter 2: The Failure Curriculum

Twenty of my thirty companies failed.

Here's something people don't say about failure: it's not the failures themselves that teach you. It's whether you can see them clearly enough to learn from them.

Most founders and executives cannot. They call failures "market timing." They call their bad hires "culture fit issues that were hard to predict." They call failed products "pivots." The language of failure in the startup world is so euphemistic that entire careers can pass without a person learning a single concrete thing from the times they got it wrong.

When I asked Behnam Bastani — CEO of OpenInfer, former AI executive at Meta — what his most important failure taught him, he said: "The company I built before OpenInfer failed because I optimized for the technology instead of the problem. We built something genuinely impressive that nobody needed. I was in love with what we could do, not what the market was asking for."

That's a failure that teaches. Notice the specificity. Notice the direct causal chain: love of technology → ignored market signal → product nobody needed → company failed.

Compare that to: "The timing wasn't right."

The timing is almost never the real reason.

**The Three Lies Software Leaders Tell About Failure**

*Lie #1: "We just needed more runway."*  
More runway would have extended the time you spent going in the wrong direction. The problem was direction, not duration.

*Lie #2: "The team wasn't strong enough."*  
In most cases the team was fine and the leadership wasn't giving them what they needed to succeed — clarity, resources, or appropriate decision-making authority.

*Lie #3: "We were ahead of our time."*  
Occasionally true. Usually not. This is the most comforting lie and therefore the most dangerous.

**The Failure Curriculum**

William Steenbergen, CTO of Federato (AI-native insurance platform), told me something I've been repeating ever since: "I treat every post-mortem like a curriculum. Not a blame session. Not a therapy session. A curriculum. What was the lesson? Is there a practice we can adopt so we don't need to relearn this?"

That's the frame. Every failure is a curriculum. The question is whether you're willing to sit in the classroom.

### Chapter 3: Trust Before Tools

Before you implement a single AI tool, before you reorganize your engineering org, before you adopt a new methodology — you need to ask a harder question:

*Do your people trust you?*

This sounds soft. It is not soft. Trust is infrastructure. You can't route work, decisions, or accountability through a team that doesn't have it.

When I talked with Todd Persen about managing a global engineering team across multiple time zones, I asked what made it work. He didn't mention Slack. He didn't mention Jira. He said: "Trust. If people believe you'll tell them the truth about what's happening — good and bad — they can self-organize around almost anything. If they don't trust you, all the tools in the world just create more overhead."

**How Trust Gets Built (and Destroyed)**

Trust between a software leader and their team is built through a surprisingly small number of mechanisms:

1. **Consistency between what you say and what you do.** If you say you value work-life balance and then Slack someone at 11pm, you've made a withdrawal from the trust account.

2. **Transparency under pressure.** When things go wrong — a missed deadline, a production incident, a budget cut — how you communicate is the whole game. Hiding, spinning, or delaying the hard conversation destroys trust faster than almost anything.

3. **Credit flows down; accountability flows up.** Leaders who take credit for the team's wins and deflect blame for failures are universally despised. Leaders who do the opposite are universally trusted.

4. **Predictability.** Your team should be able to predict how you'll respond to a given situation. Not because you're robotic — because you've been consistent enough that they have a model of you they can work with.

5. **Follow-through on small things.** You said you'd review the PR by Friday. Review it by Friday. The small things are actually the big things.

**The Trust Audit**

Here's an uncomfortable exercise. Imagine your two best engineers were having an honest conversation about you over lunch, out of earshot. What would they say?

If you don't know the answer, that's information.

If you suspect the answer and don't like it, that's a mandate to change.

### Chapter 4: Hiring Is a Strategy, Not a Checklist

The most expensive thing a software leader does is make a bad hire.

Not because of the salary. Because of the opportunity cost — the six to twelve months you spend managing someone out, the team energy that gets absorbed by the dysfunction, the work that doesn't get done, the good people who leave because they don't want to work next to someone who shouldn't be there.

Dom Scandinaro, CTO of Cameo, ran product and engineering through some of the most intense growth and contraction cycles the consumer tech space has seen. When I asked him what he'd do differently on hiring, he didn't hesitate: "I would have been more disciplined about defining the role before I interviewed for it. We hired for who impressed us in the room, not who the role actually needed. Those are different things."

**Define the Role, Then Interview**

Most hiring starts with a job description copy-pasted from the last time you hired for this function, updated with whatever framework or tool is currently trending. That's not role definition. That's theater.

Real role definition answers:
- What is the most important outcome this person needs to drive in their first 90 days?
- What decisions will this person make independently?
- What does great look like in this role, specifically, not generically?
- What's hard about this role that a candidate needs to be genuinely prepared for?

Interview for those things. Not for abstract "culture fit" (a term so vague it's functionally useless) and not for whether you'd enjoy getting a beer with someone (an excellent way to accidentally build a monoculture).

**The Technical Interview Trap**

I've watched engineering teams run technical interviews that perfectly assess algorithmic problem-solving ability and tell you almost nothing about whether someone can actually do the job.

The best engineers I know — and the best engineering leaders — have a mixed record on whiteboard exercises. And the best whiteboard performers I've seen have sometimes been terrible at actual product engineering work.

Michael Cieri, a product development leader who appeared on the podcast, said it plainly: "AI helps, but the fundamentals win. And the fundamentals of engineering aren't LeetCode problems. They're: can this person take a messy problem, get clarity on what matters, and ship something that works? Test for that."

**Interview Design for What Actually Matters**

Replace: "Reverse a linked list on this whiteboard."  
With: "Here's a real architecture decision we made six months ago. What questions would you ask before you made the call? What tradeoffs do you see?"

Replace: "Tell me about a time you worked with a difficult team member."  
With: "Walk me through a decision you made that you later disagreed with. What changed?"

These are harder questions to answer and harder questions to grade. That's the point.

### Chapter 5: The Nearshore Advantage (and the Nearshore Trap)

I built Sonatafy on the premise that nearshore software development — specifically, elite engineering talent from Mexico and Latin America working in U.S. time zones — could change how American companies build software.

I still believe that. And I've also seen it done badly enough to give me a list of warnings.

Let's start with why it works when it works.

**Why Nearshore Works**

Nearshoring, done well, is not about cost. That's the trap most companies fall into when they start the conversation. They look at the hourly rate differential and treat it as an arbitrage opportunity. They hire cheap, manage poorly, and then declare that "offshore doesn't work" — when what they built was offshore in a nearshore wrapper.

Nearshoring works because:

1. **Time zone alignment.** A developer in Mexico City or Guadalajara is working the same hours you are. This means real-time collaboration, the ability to have a conversation rather than an async message, and the elimination of the overnight handoff problem that makes traditional offshore development painful.

2. **Cultural proximity.** Years of cross-border business, education, and cultural exchange mean that working norms, communication styles, and professional expectations are closer than most people assume.

3. **Talent concentration.** The engineering talent in major Latin American tech hubs is genuinely world-class. Not "world-class for the price." World-class.

When I talked with Chad Smykay — AI CTO at Hewlett Packard Enterprise, a man who has managed engineering organizations across multiple continents — about what separates high-performing distributed teams from low-performing ones, he said: "It's never about geography. It's about whether the team has the same information, the same clarity about the goal, and the same trust in the people making decisions."

That's the work. And it's the same work whether your team is across the street or across the border.

**The Nearshore Trap**

Here's where companies go wrong:

*They set lower expectations.* This is both disrespectful and counterproductive. If you assume nearshore engineers need more oversight, more hand-holding, more checking — you'll create the conditions for exactly that. Hire great people, give them what they need to succeed, and hold them to the same bar you'd hold anyone.

*They under-invest in onboarding.* The first 30 days of any engineering engagement — nearshore or not — determine most of the outcome. If you spend those 30 days getting someone access to the right systems, explaining the business context, and introducing them to the team properly, you'll get months of payoff. If you throw them a Jira ticket and say "figure it out," you'll spend months recovering.

*They hire for cost and manage for cost.* The question is never "how cheap can this be?" The question is "what level of talent do we need to accomplish this, and what's the most effective way to access that talent?" Sometimes the answer is nearshore. Sometimes it's local. Sometimes it's a hybrid. Cost is a factor, not the frame.

### Chapter 6: Remote Culture Is Not an Accident

Most remote team culture is not designed. It emerges — usually badly — from the accumulated micro-decisions of a thousand daily interactions.

Someone puts a Slack message in the wrong channel. Nobody corrects it. That becomes the channel now. The on-call rotation was never written down. It exists in someone's head. That person leaves. Chaos. The Friday standup started as a "quick check-in" and is now an hour and a half of everyone summarizing what they did all week. Nobody cancels it because that would require a conversation about why.

This is how remote culture happens without intention: by default, not design.

**What Intentional Remote Culture Looks Like**

Jonathan LaCour, who built and scaled an effective remote team at Mission Cloud, described his approach: "Everything that matters has to be written down. Not because I don't trust people to remember — because memory is lossy and writing is permanent. The culture of the team should be recoverable by a new person reading the documentation. If it's not recoverable, it's fragile."

That's a high bar. And it's the right bar.

Intentional remote culture requires:

**Documentation as a first-class discipline.** Not just technical documentation — cultural documentation. How do we make decisions? Who is responsible for what? What's the process for raising a concern? How do we handle disagreements? If the answer to any of these questions is "we just know" or "it depends on the situation" — you're one departure away from a crisis.

**Async by default, sync by exception.** Most information transfer does not require a meeting. Most alignment can be achieved through a well-written document, a recorded video, or a structured Slack thread. Meetings should be reserved for the things that genuinely require real-time interaction: creative problem-solving, emotionally sensitive conversations, and complex decision-making with multiple stakeholders.

**Explicit recognition, since accidental recognition is rare.** In an office, you see someone go above and beyond and you can acknowledge it in the moment. Remote removes the casual observation. You have to build systems for recognition or it won't happen. Not performative public praise for everything — genuine, specific acknowledgment when someone does something that matters.

**Rituals that create belonging.** Teams need moments that feel like shared experience. A weekly show-and-tell. A Slack channel for non-work conversation that people actually use. A quarterly in-person that's worth the travel. The specifics matter less than the consistency.

### Chapter 7: When the Team Breaks Down

Every engineering team breaks down eventually. The question is whether the breakdown is a crisis or a curriculum.

Dan Sloan has spent his career fixing broken systems — specifically, broken ERP implementations and the organizational dysfunction that surrounds them. When I talked with him, he made a point that I've thought about constantly since: "The technology is almost never the problem. The process breaks the technology. The culture breaks the process. The leader breaks the culture."

That chain — leader → culture → process → technology — is the right direction of causality. Almost every "technology problem" I've encountered in my career was upstream a people problem dressed in technical clothing.

**Signs of a Breaking Team**

Watch for these:

- Engineers stop raising concerns in group settings (but raise them in 1:1s, or in hallways, or not at all)
- The ratio of meetings to decisions is increasing (more alignment work needed because alignment is eroding)
- High performers start interviewing elsewhere
- Post-mortems become blame sessions
- The roadmap keeps changing but nobody can explain why
- "Technical debt" is a constant topic in leadership conversations but never gets addressed

**The Intervention**

When a team is breaking down, the instinct is often to add process — more standups, more documentation, more reviews. This is usually the wrong instinct.

Breaking teams typically need clarity, not process. They need to know what they're building, why they're building it, how their work connects to something that matters, and what the rules of engagement are when things go sideways.

Bradley Friemel, Vertical CTO at Fullsteam, operates in a private equity environment where teams get acquired, integrated, and reorganized constantly. He talked about entering a new engineering org: "The first thing I do is not change anything. I try to understand what's working and why. Because in a dysfunctional team, there's always something that's working — and if you blow that up while trying to fix the broken stuff, you've made it worse."

That restraint — the discipline to understand before acting — is one of the most underrated leadership skills there is.

### Chapter 8: Product and Engineering Are Not the Same Team

This is the source of more organizational suffering than almost any other structural issue in software companies.

Product managers and engineers are trained differently, rewarded differently, and — if leadership isn't careful — working toward different definitions of success. Product is measured on outcomes: user adoption, revenue impact, feature usage. Engineering is often measured on outputs: tickets closed, sprints completed, code shipped. These are not the same things. And when the measurement systems diverge, the teams diverge.

Jeff Prince, CTO of XOi (a field service intelligence platform), talked about this on the podcast in a way that cut to the heart of it: "The best thing I ever did for product-engineering alignment was to stop letting the two teams have separate roadmaps. One roadmap. One set of priorities. Both teams own it. The moment you have two roadmaps, you have two cultures, and eventually two companies fighting inside one building."

One roadmap. One set of priorities. Both teams own it.

This is easier to say than to implement. Here's what implementation requires:

**Shared language.** Product people talk about user journeys, personas, and conversion funnels. Engineering people talk about latency, architecture, and technical debt. Neither group is wrong. Both groups need to be able to talk in each other's language — or at minimum, a shared language that belongs to neither.

**Engineering at the table earlier.** The most expensive place to discover a technical constraint is after the product spec is written, the design is done, and the sprint is planned. Engineers need to be in the room when the "what are we building next?" conversation happens — not as reviewers of a decision already made, but as contributors to the thinking.

**Product people who understand the cost of what they're asking.** You don't need a product manager who can write code. You need a product manager who understands that "just add a filter" is not always a small thing, and who can have an informed conversation with engineering about what tradeoffs they're asking for.

**Shared accountability for outcomes.** When a feature ships and nobody adopts it, who owns that? If the answer is "product," engineering has no incentive to care about adoption. If the answer is shared, suddenly the conversation before launch changes.

### Chapter 9: Legacy Code Is a Leadership Problem

Every CTO who has inherited an existing engineering organization has faced this moment: you open the codebase for the first time, and you realize the architecture is held together by hope, heroics, and a 2008 PHP script that everyone is afraid to touch.

Legacy code is real. Technical debt is real. And both of them are regularly used as excuses for slow delivery that have nothing to do with the code and everything to do with how the team is organized around the code.

Bradley Friemel, who manages multiple SaaS verticals at Fullsteam, described the frame he uses: "Legacy code is not the problem. Undocumented legacy code is a problem. Untested legacy code is a problem. Legacy code that nobody on the team understands is a crisis. The code being old is actually fine — old code that works is valuable code."

This reframe matters enormously.

**The Strategic Approach to Technical Debt**

The question is not "when do we rewrite everything?" (Almost never. Rewrites fail more often than not.) The question is "which pieces of the existing system are actively preventing us from delivering value, and what's the smallest intervention that creates the most leverage?"

This is a business question, not a technical question. It requires knowing:
- Which systems are on the critical path of your most important product work?
- Where are engineers spending time navigating complexity rather than creating value?
- What's the cost of a production incident in this area, and how often are we paying it?

Dan Sloan's work fixing broken ERP systems taught him something about this: "People treat ERP failures like technology failures. They're almost never technology failures. They're implementation failures. The system can do what you need it to do — the problem is nobody sat down long enough to understand the process before they tried to automate it."

Process before automation. This is the recurring lesson. It applies to legacy code, to AI implementation, to almost everything.

**The Boy Scout Rule at Scale**

Leave the codebase slightly better than you found it. Not as a massive initiative. Not as a rewrite project with a six-month timeline and a project manager. As a practice. Every time someone opens a file to make a change, they fix one thing nearby that they notice is wrong.

At scale, the Boy Scout Rule is surprisingly powerful. It distributes the improvement work across every sprint, prevents the debt from compounding, and keeps engineers engaged with code quality as a continuous concern rather than a quarterly crisis.

### Chapter 10: The Delivery Discipline

Software that is almost done is not done. It is expensive inventory that has not yet generated any value.

This is one of the most counterintuitive ideas in software leadership, and one of the most important.

Todd Persen at Hydrolix built his engineering culture around shortening release cycles — the time between when something is done and when it's in front of users. The reason, he explained, is not just speed. It's feedback. "Every day a feature sits in the release queue is a day you're not learning whether you got it right. And in software, you almost never get it completely right the first time. The shorter your cycle, the faster you can correct."

This is not an argument for shipping broken software. It's an argument for the smallest unit of releasable value — the thinnest slice that actually works and actually teaches you something — over the complete perfect feature that ships six months late and turns out to solve a problem that has already evolved.

**What Slows Delivery**

The usual suspects:
- Features that are too big (nobody ever decomposed them into shippable slices)
- Handoffs between teams with no ownership model
- Code review as a bottleneck (not enough reviewers, not clear enough review criteria, not fast enough turnaround)
- Testing that runs in three hours (or not at all)
- Deployment that requires a manual process and an on-call hero

All of these are fixable. None of them are fixed with urgency alone. They're fixed with systems.

## PART FOUR: THE INTELLIGENCE
### AI, Cybersecurity, and the Transformation That's Already Happening

---

### Chapter 11: AI Is Not the Strategy

Every week I talk to a CTO who is trying to figure out their "AI strategy." And almost every week, I tell them the same thing:

AI is not a strategy. It is a capability. The strategy is what you're trying to accomplish for your customers. AI is one of the tools.

This distinction matters because companies with "AI strategies" often end up with impressive-sounding AI initiatives that don't connect to customer outcomes. And companies that treat AI as a capability in service of a real strategy end up with tools that work.

Chad Smykay at Hewlett Packard Enterprise has deployed AI at enterprise scale — the kind of scale where a percentage point of efficiency improvement is worth tens of millions of dollars. When I asked him what the companies that do AI well have in common, he said: "They start with the workflow. What is the human doing today? Where is the human spending time on something a machine could do better or faster? Start there. Don't start with the model."

Start with the workflow. Not the model.

**Where AI Actually Works in Engineering Teams**

Based on my conversations with over 188 software leaders, here's where AI integration is generating real value:

*Code completion and review.* AI-assisted coding tools (you know the names) genuinely accelerate certain types of implementation work. The caveat that every honest engineer I've talked to includes: "It makes me faster at writing code. It does not replace judgment about what code to write."

*Prototyping and exploration.* Bradley Friemel at Fullsteam specifically mentioned AI-assisted prototyping as a place where the technology has changed the game. "I can explore a solution space in an afternoon that used to take a week. The prototype isn't production code, but it tells me whether the idea is worth pursuing."

*Incident management.* Sylvain Kalache, head of AI Labs at Rootly AI, has built an entire company around AI's ability to accelerate incident response. The time from alert to root cause identification has dropped dramatically for teams that use AI-augmented incident management. Not eliminated — AI gets it wrong, and humans need to verify — but dramatically accelerated.

*Documentation.* This one's quiet but significant. AI tools that can generate first drafts of technical documentation from code, from meeting transcripts, from architectural decision records — they are changing how teams maintain institutional knowledge. Imperfectly. But meaningfully.

**Where AI Does Not Work (Yet)**

*Architecture decisions.* AI can help you think through tradeoffs. It cannot replace the judgment of a senior engineer who understands your specific context, your team's capabilities, your technical debt, and your business constraints.

*Judgment about what to build.* An AI tool can analyze usage data and generate a list of potential features. It cannot tell you which one of those features will actually matter to your most important customers in a way that changes their behavior.

*The human moments.* A team in conflict, a key engineer who's about to quit, a stakeholder who doesn't trust the engineering team — none of these are AI problems. They are human problems that require human presence.

### Chapter 12: AI-Native Products Are Different

William Steenbergen at Federato is building what he calls an AI-native insurance platform — not a traditional insurance platform with AI bolted on, but a system designed from the ground up around AI's capabilities.

The difference is fundamental and worth understanding.

A traditional software product treats AI as a feature. An AI-native product treats AI as infrastructure. The user experience, the data model, the business logic — all of it is designed around the assumption that AI is doing work that used to be human work, not assisting humans with work that remains human.

This distinction creates entirely different engineering challenges.

**Engineering for AI-Native Products**

*Data quality becomes existential.* In traditional software, bad data is a problem. In AI-native software, bad data is a catastrophe, because the AI's output quality is directly tied to the quality of what you put in. Steenbergen talked about this: "We spend more engineering time on data quality infrastructure than almost anything else. Because if the data is wrong, everything is wrong."

*Explainability is a product requirement.* When an AI makes a decision in a regulated industry (insurance, finance, healthcare), the human in the loop needs to be able to understand why. This is not a nice-to-have. It's a regulatory requirement and a trust requirement. Engineering explainability into AI systems is genuinely hard and frequently underestimated.

*The feedback loop is the product.* The AI gets better as it gets more data. Which means the product gets better as it gets more usage. Which means the business case for AI-native products is that the moat grows with every customer interaction. This is structurally different from traditional SaaS, and it changes how you think about pricing, onboarding, and long-term competitive advantage.

**Behnam Bastani on Edge AI**

Behnam Bastani founded OpenInfer to solve what he sees as the next frontier of AI deployment: running AI models at the edge, on-device, without requiring a cloud connection for every inference. When I talked with him about what this means for software leaders, he was expansive:

"Most of the AI conversation is about the model — GPT this, Claude that. The interesting engineering problem is inference. How do you run sophisticated AI fast, cheaply, and locally? Because the use cases that are going to matter most in the next decade — autonomous vehicles, medical devices, industrial systems — cannot have a round-trip to the cloud in the loop."

This is an important signal. The infrastructure of AI is still being built. The leaders who understand inference, not just training, are going to have an enormous advantage.

### Chapter 13: Security Is Not Someone Else's Job

Michael Lieberman, CTO of Kusari, has spent his career on a problem that most engineering teams are underinvesting in: software supply chain security.

The attack surface of modern software has expanded dramatically. You're not just securing your code — you're securing every library you depend on, every container you pull, every third-party service you integrate. The SolarWinds attack. The Log4j vulnerability. The XZ Utils backdoor. These are supply chain attacks, and they work because the industry has spent twenty years building software that depends on dependencies without asking hard questions about what those dependencies contain.

Lieberman's term for one emerging threat is "AI slop squatting" — where AI-generated code suggests the use of packages that don't exist, and attackers pre-register those package names to serve malicious code to any developer who follows the AI's suggestion.

This is not a hypothetical future risk. It is happening now.

**What Security-Aware Engineering Looks Like**

Jason Birmingham, CTO at Broadridge, manages engineering at financial infrastructure scale — the kind of scale where a security failure isn't just a PR problem, it's a systemic risk to financial markets. He outlined the core practices:

*Software Bill of Materials (SBOM).* Know what's in your software. Every dependency. Every version. Not just your first-level dependencies — the whole tree. You cannot secure what you cannot see.

*Shift left on security.* Security testing in CI/CD, not as a post-deployment audit. The earlier in the development cycle you find a vulnerability, the cheaper it is to fix.

*Zero trust architecture.* Every service, every request, authenticated and authorized. Not "authenticated if it's on the corporate network." Always authenticated. Always authorized.

*Incident response drills.* You will have an incident. The question is whether you've practiced the response before the crisis, or whether you're improvising during it. Companies that drill incident response — tabletop exercises, red team exercises, chaos engineering — handle real incidents dramatically better than those that don't.

*Culture of security ownership.* Security is not the security team's job. It's every engineer's job. The security team's job is to create the standards, the tooling, and the education that enables every engineer to own security in their work. This cultural shift is harder than any technical implementation, and it's the most important one.

## PART FIVE: THE FUTURE
### What Software Leadership Looks Like in the Age That's Coming

---

### Chapter 14: The Human Advantage

There is an enormous amount of anxiety in the engineering community about AI taking engineering jobs.

I want to offer a different frame.

The best engineers I know — the ones who will thrive in an AI-augmented world — are not better than AI at writing code. They're better than AI at the things that matter:

Understanding the human context that software exists in. The political dynamics between departments that determine whether a system will be adopted. The customer behavior that isn't in the data because customers haven't told anyone about it yet. The implicit constraints that live in the heads of three engineers who've been at the company for eight years.

Asking the questions that reveal what the real problem is. AI can answer almost any question you give it. The skill is knowing which question to ask. And knowing which question to ask requires the kind of contextual, cross-domain, human-experience-informed judgment that machines don't have and won't have soon.

Making the calls that require moral courage. Do we ship with this known bug because the business needs the revenue? Do we tell the customer that their timeline is impossible even though they're not going to like hearing it? Do we push back on the executive's feature idea even though it's going to be an uncomfortable conversation? These are not AI calls. They are human calls.

Nick Gattuso, building the engineering team at Somethings (a mental health platform for teens), talked about this when I asked how AI changes the engineering job: "In mental health tech, the human judgment is the whole point. AI helps me analyze patterns, identify anomalies, accelerate certain workflows. But the decision about how a product interacts with a vulnerable teenager? That's a human decision. It should be a human decision."

The human advantage is real. But it requires investment.

**Developing the Skills AI Can't Replace**

Judgment improves with deliberate practice and honest feedback. If you're not putting yourself in situations where you have to make hard calls — and then examining what happened — your judgment isn't growing.

Communication ability is the multiplier. A software leader who can communicate clearly, concisely, and persuasively to both technical and non-technical audiences is worth multiples of one who can't. This is learnable, but it requires practice.

Systems thinking — the ability to see how changes in one part of a system create effects in other parts — is increasingly valuable in an AI-augmented world where the systems are getting more complex. Reading broadly, working across domains, studying failure modes: these are the inputs.

Emotional intelligence. The ability to read a room, navigate conflict, give feedback that lands, and maintain relationships under pressure. AI will not replace this. The leaders who develop it will have an extraordinary advantage.

### Chapter 15: What the Next Ten Years Require

I've had 188 conversations with the people building the software future. Here's what I believe the next decade requires of software leaders.

**Adaptability as a Core Competency**

The shelf life of any specific technical skill is shorter than it's ever been. What makes engineers and engineering leaders valuable over a career is not expertise in any particular technology. It's the ability to learn rapidly, adapt intelligently, and apply judgment across changing contexts.

This sounds obvious. It is not practiced.

Most engineering cultures reward depth — the person who knows one thing exceptionally well. The next decade will also reward breadth — the person who can learn a new domain quickly and apply patterns from elsewhere.

**The Generalist-Specialist Balance**

The best CTOs I know have genuine depth in one or two domains and functional literacy across many. They can have a real conversation about machine learning without being an ML engineer. They can understand the cybersecurity implications of an architecture decision without being a security specialist. They can engage with a product roadmap without being a product manager.

This is not about being a jack of all trades. It's about having enough context in adjacent domains that your judgment in your own domain is better informed.

**Building Organizations That Learn**

The most durable competitive advantage a software company can have is an engineering organization that learns faster than the competition.

Not just that ships faster — that learns faster. That treats every sprint as a source of data about what's working. That does post-mortems not as blame-finding exercises but as genuine curriculum. That creates the conditions for feedback — from customers, from the market, from internal retrospectives — to actually change how work gets done.

Wendy Dole, Director of Engineering at Direct Supply (which builds technology for senior living healthcare), described what this looks like in practice: "We're in a domain where the technology is changing almost as fast as the care models it serves. We can't just be good at building — we have to be good at learning what to build. That's a different skill. And it requires a culture that treats uncertainty as the normal state rather than as a failure mode."

Uncertainty as the normal state. That's the frame.

**The Relationship Between Speed and Quality**

There is a persistent belief in engineering culture that speed and quality are in tension — that you can move fast or you can do it right, but not both.

This is false, and the best engineering organizations I've seen operate as living proof of it.

Speed comes from clarity: clear requirements, clear ownership, clear acceptance criteria, clear process for getting unstuck. When those things are in place, the work moves fast.

Quality comes from discipline: automated testing, code review standards, deployment guardrails, monitoring and alerting. When those systems are in place, the quality holds even as velocity increases.

The companies that experience quality degrading as they scale are almost always missing one or both of these: clarity or discipline. The fix is not to slow down. It's to build the infrastructure of quality and clarity that lets you go fast safely.

## CONCLUSION: THE UNCENSORED TRUTH

After 188 conversations, thousands of hours, and the accumulated experience of the best software leaders I know, here is what I believe the uncensored truth of software leadership is:

**The technology is almost never the problem.**

The problem is almost always: clarity, trust, alignment, culture, or courage.

Clarity about what you're building and why.

Trust between the people doing the building.

Alignment between the teams responsible for different parts of the work.

Culture that rewards learning, honesty, and high standards — not the appearance of these things.

Courage to have the hard conversations, make the difficult calls, and tell the truth even when the truth is inconvenient.

Every technical challenge I've seen — legacy code, AI implementation, security vulnerabilities, scaling failures, product-engineering misalignment — had a human root. And every human challenge I've seen was solvable by a leader willing to do the unglamorous work: listen, reflect, communicate, decide, act, and learn.

That's the playbook. It's not complicated. It's just hard.

And that's exactly what makes it worth doing.
